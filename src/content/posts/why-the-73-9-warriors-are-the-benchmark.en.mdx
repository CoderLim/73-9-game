The name **73-9** is not a random score. It is the regular-season record of the 2015-16 Golden State Warriors and remains the highest win total in NBA history.

That achievement gives the game a clear target. A lineup does not merely need to look impressive; it must be measured against a team whose regular-season performance already established an extreme historical standard.

The record is also only part of the story, which is why it makes a better game benchmark than a simple declaration of the “greatest team ever.”

## The record

Golden State finished the 2015-16 regular season with 73 wins and 9 losses. The team passed the 1995-96 Chicago Bulls, who had gone 72-10.

The NBA’s official season review describes the Warriors’ 73 wins as the best regular-season record in league history. The previous Bulls record had stood for twenty years.

One additional win may look like a small difference, but improving an already dominant record is difficult. An 82-game season includes travel, injuries, schedule variation, back-to-backs, and opponents with different styles. Reaching 73 required sustained performance from October through April.

## The 24-0 start

Golden State opened the season with 24 consecutive victories.

An undefeated start changes the pressure around every game. Opponents treat the matchup as an opportunity to stop the streak, media attention grows, and each win extends an achievement that has no guaranteed endpoint.

The 24-0 opening was one of several records connected to the season. It also established the pace required to threaten Chicago’s 72 wins long before the final week.

## The road record

The Warriors won 34 road games, another NBA record at the time.

Road performance matters because home-court familiarity, travel, crowd environment, and routine generally make away games more difficult. A historic overall record supported by historic road success is stronger evidence of sustained dominance than a season built almost entirely at home.

Golden State also completed the regular season without consecutive losses and without losing twice to the same opponent, according to the NBA’s MVP announcement.

## Stephen Curry’s season

Stephen Curry became the first unanimous Most Valuable Player in NBA history. He received all 131 first-place votes.

Curry led the league in scoring at 30.1 points per game, made an NBA-record 402 three-pointers, led the league in free-throw percentage, and averaged 2.14 steals. He shot 50.4 percent from the field and 45.4 percent from three-point range.

The 402 figure mattered beyond the record itself. Curry became the first player to make more than 400 threes in a season. His shooting range and volume changed the geometry of normal possessions and forced defenses to react far from the basket.

The game does not attempt to recreate every tactical consequence of that shooting. The historical season still provides the identity of the benchmark: elite team performance centered on an unprecedented offensive season.

## More than one player

The Warriors were not a one-player roster. Klay Thompson provided high-volume shooting and perimeter defense. Draymond Green contributed playmaking, defense, rebounding, and lineup flexibility. Andre Iguodala, Harrison Barnes, Andrew Bogut, Shaun Livingston, and other rotation players gave the team multiple ways to play.

This matters for the 73-9 challenge. A lineup built only around one expensive scorer may have star power but lack the five-player depth associated with the benchmark.

The game’s budget is designed to make that depth difficult to purchase. It is easy to choose a great first player. It is harder to build five connected positions without exhausting the cap.

## The 72-10 Bulls comparison

The 1995-96 Chicago Bulls went 72-10 and won the NBA championship. Michael Jordan won the regular-season, All-Star Game, and Finals MVP awards. The Bulls completed the playoffs 15-3.

The Warriors passed Chicago’s regular-season win total but did not finish the postseason with a championship. Cleveland came back from a 3-1 deficit in the 2016 Finals.

That difference prevents the number 73 from closing the historical argument. Fans who prioritize the best regular-season record can point to Golden State. Fans who require the championship can point to Chicago’s complete season.

The 73-9 game does not need to resolve the debate. It needs a difficult, understandable target. Golden State supplies that target while leaving room for discussion about what team greatness means.

## Why not use a championship team instead?

A title is the ultimate objective of an NBA season, but championships are not a single numerical benchmark. Playoff paths, injuries, matchups, series length, and conference strength vary.

The 73-9 record provides a precise wall that can be used in a game interface. It tells the player exactly what historical achievement inspired the challenge.

The simulation still includes a broader 82-game result, while the Warriors comparison focuses the final question.

## Why the target remains useful

A useful benchmark should be:

- Recognizable to basketball fans.
- Difficult enough that most random lineups fail.
- Connected to a complete team rather than one player.
- Supported by reliable historical records.
- Open to discussion rather than pretending to be an absolute truth.

The 2015-16 Warriors meet those conditions.

Their season combines the highest win total, an unprecedented MVP vote, record three-point shooting, a 24-0 start, and historic road performance. The Finals loss adds complexity instead of erasing the regular-season achievement.

## How the game uses the history

73-9 converts the benchmark into a salary-cap problem. You receive random team-seasons, select one player from each, and try to create enough five-player strength under $100M.

The game’s ratings, prices, and simulated percentages are independent mechanics. NBA historical facts establish the context; they do not validate every model output.

A result should therefore be read as: “How did this lineup perform against the 73-9 benchmark inside the game?” It should not be read as a definitive real-world answer about cross-era basketball.
