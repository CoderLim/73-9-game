The **73-9 Warriors** did more than break a regular-season record. They made the rest of the league reconsider where shots should come from, how much space an offense could create, which players could function as playmakers, and how quickly a championship roster needed to adapt.

Golden State finished the 2015-16 season with 73 wins and 9 losses, passing the 1995-96 Chicago Bulls’ 72-10 mark. Stephen Curry became the first unanimous NBA Most Valuable Player and made 402 three-pointers. The **73-9 Warriors** opened 24-0 and won 34 road games.

Those records matter, but the lasting influence of the **73-9 Warriors** is visible in the decisions teams made afterward. The **73-9 Warriors** became a reference point for every contender trying to modernize its offense and defense.

## The 73-9 Warriors expanded the court

NBA teams used the three-point line long before 2015-16. The **73-9 Warriors** changed the scale and geometry of the threat.

Curry did not need to stand directly behind the line to create spacing. His comfortable shooting range pulled defenders several steps away from the basket. That extra distance opened driving lanes, passing windows, slips, cuts, and four-on-three situations after traps.

The **73-9 Warriors** showed that spacing was not only about placing several average shooters around the arc. One extraordinary shooter could change the location of the entire defense.

Klay Thompson added a different kind of pressure. He could score without holding the ball for long, sprint through screens, punish late help, and defend perimeter scorers. The combination meant the **73-9 Warriors** could create elite shooting pressure both on and off the ball.

After the **73-9 Warriors**, front offices placed even greater value on guards and wings whose range forced defenses to react early. The **73-9 Warriors** made shooting range a roster-building skill, not merely an individual highlight.

## Three-point volume became a strategic requirement

Curry’s 402 three-pointers were an individual record, but the larger lesson was about expected value and volume.

The **73-9 Warriors** demonstrated that a great offense could take large numbers of threes without becoming predictable. The shots came from transition, movement, pick-and-rolls, handoffs, broken plays, and quick passing.

Teams did not copy every action exactly. They copied the principle that three-point attempts could be a central offensive resource rather than a secondary option.

The influence of the **73-9 Warriors** accelerated a league-wide shift. The **73-9 Warriors** gave teams a visible model for turning three-point volume into an entire offensive identity. Teams searched for more shooting at every position, encouraged quicker decisions, and treated poor spacing as a structural roster problem.

A center or power forward who could not shoot needed to create value through screening, passing, offensive rebounding, rim pressure, or elite defense. The **73-9 Warriors** raised the cost of playing multiple non-shooters together.

## Draymond Green changed the idea of a playmaker

The **73-9 Warriors** were built around Curry, but Draymond Green made many of their most difficult lineups possible.

Green could rebound, push the ball, initiate offense, pass from the top of the floor, screen, defend several positions, and protect the rim in smaller groups. He allowed the **73-9 Warriors** to use a frontcourt player as a decision-maker rather than only a finisher.

When defenses trapped Curry, Green often received the ball with a numbers advantage. His ability to attack four-on-three situations punished the strategy used to remove the primary scorer.

The league increasingly valued players who could connect possessions. These players might not lead a team in points, but they could pass, screen, defend, and make the next decision before the defense recovered.

The **73-9 Warriors** made “playmaking big” and “short-roll decision-maker” common parts of roster conversations.

## Small-ball stopped looking small

The closing lineup associated with the **73-9 Warriors** placed Green at center with Curry, Thompson, Andre Iguodala, and Harrison Barnes.

That group was smaller than many traditional lineups, but it did not play weak. The **73-9 Warriors** used speed, switching, help defense, anticipation, and collective rebounding to offset the size difference.

The important lesson was not “always remove the center.” The lesson was that functional size matters more than listed position.

Green could defend larger players. Iguodala and Barnes could switch across the perimeter. Thompson could handle difficult guard assignments. The **73-9 Warriors** had enough strength and length to survive while gaining speed and spacing.

After the **73-9 Warriors**, more teams searched for centers who could defend in space and wings who could move up a position without becoming defensive targets. The **73-9 Warriors** made lineup flexibility feel like a requirement rather than a luxury.

## Switching became part of the offensive arms race

Spacing and switching influenced each other.

As offenses created more space, defenses needed players who could survive away from their normal assignments. The **73-9 Warriors** could switch many actions, reduce the advantage created by screens, and recover without immediately sending extra help.

This encouraged opponents to search for mismatches, isolation scorers, and centers who could punish smaller defenders. In response, roster building became more focused on versatility.

The **73-9 Warriors** did not invent switching defense, but their success made versatility feel essential for teams trying to survive deep in the playoffs.

A player who could not shoot and could not defend in space became harder to keep on the court. The **73-9 Warriors** helped expose both limitations at once.

## Pace did not require careless basketball

The **73-9 Warriors** were dangerous before defenses were organized, but their transition offense was not only fast breaks after steals.

Curry could pull up from three before a defense reached the paint. Thompson could run directly into a shooting action. Green could rebound and become the ball handler without waiting for a guard.

The **73-9 Warriors** compressed the time defenders had to make decisions. A defense that normally prioritized protecting the rim could not automatically retreat when the ball handler was a threat from several feet beyond the line.

Modern teams increasingly use early-clock threes, drag screens, and transition spacing for the same reason: attacking before all five defenders are connected creates better decisions.

## Regular-season excellence became part of the debate

The **73-9 Warriors** created an unusual historical problem. They produced the best regular-season record and then lost the NBA Finals after leading the series 3-1.

That ending prevents a simple conclusion. The **73-9 Warriors** can be described as the most successful regular-season team by wins, but not as the champion of their own season.

The contrast changed how fans discuss greatness. Is the best team the one with the highest regular-season peak, the championship, the strongest playoff run, or the best combination of all three?

The **73-9 Warriors** made the regular season impossible to dismiss while also demonstrating why postseason results remain decisive.

The 1995-96 Bulls finished 72-10 and won the title. The **73-9 Warriors** finished one win better and did not. That comparison will remain part of basketball history because neither side of the argument can erase the other.

## Every contender needed more lineup answers

Teams trying to beat the **73-9 Warriors** could not rely on one defensive coverage or one preferred lineup.

Switching could reduce movement but create isolation opportunities. Trapping Curry could produce a four-on-three for Green. Playing a traditional big could protect the rim but create space for pull-up shooting. Going small could improve mobility but increase rebounding and interior risk.

The **73-9 Warriors** forced opponents to prepare multiple answers. Preparing for the **73-9 Warriors** meant building a rotation that could survive more than one style. That pressure influenced how contenders built playoff rotations: more versatile wings, more shooting, more secondary playmaking, and fewer players with only one usable role.

A team no longer needed only a good starting five. It needed lineups for different matchups.

## The influence reached basketball culture

The **73-9 Warriors** changed what young players practiced and what fans celebrated.

Deep pull-up threes became a normal highlight rather than an automatic bad shot. Movement shooting received more attention. Fans began discussing gravity, spacing, switching, and lineup fit with greater frequency.

The **73-9 Warriors** also helped make roster-building tools and lineup games more interesting. A five-player challenge now had to ask more than “Who are the five biggest stars?” It had to ask who creates space, who makes decisions, who defends multiple positions, and who remains effective without the ball.

That is why the **73-9 Warriors** work as the benchmark for 73-9.org. Their strength was not one isolated rating. It was the interaction of shooting, playmaking, pace, defense, and lineup flexibility.

## What teams could not simply copy

Many teams tried to imitate the **73-9 Warriors** by increasing three-point attempts. The harder parts were not as easy to reproduce.

Curry’s range was unique. Thompson combined shooting with perimeter defense. Green combined defense with playmaking. Iguodala accepted a changing role while remaining valuable in high-leverage lineups.

The **73-9 Warriors** were not a template that could be rebuilt by signing any five shooters. Their system depended on specific skills, timing, trust, and decision speed.

The lasting lesson is not “shoot like Golden State.” It is “build a roster whose skills multiply one another.”

## Why the 73-9 Warriors still matter

The league has continued to change, but the core questions raised by the **73-9 Warriors** remain current:

- How much shooting is enough?
- Can the center defend in space?
- Who makes decisions when the star is trapped?
- Which players stay useful without the ball?
- Can the lineup change styles without substituting?
- Is a record-setting regular season complete without a title?

The **73-9 Warriors** remain a useful historical benchmark because they combined measurable dominance with unresolved debate. The **73-9 Warriors** changed the league while leaving the final argument open.
