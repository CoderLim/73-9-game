What does **73-9** mean in basketball? The number refers to the Golden State Warriors’ 2015-16 regular-season record: 73 wins and 9 losses. That **73-9** finish gave Golden State the highest regular-season win total in NBA history, one victory more than the 72-10 Chicago Bulls of 1995-96.

The story of **73-9** is bigger than one standings line. It includes Stephen Curry’s unanimous MVP season, a 24-0 start, record road success, a style that accelerated the league’s three-point revolution, and a Finals loss that keeps the season open to debate. Understanding **73-9** means understanding both unprecedented dominance and an unfinished ending.

## What is 73-9?

**73-9** is shorthand for the Warriors winning 73 of 82 regular-season games and losing only 9 during the 2015-16 NBA season. The record produced an .890 winning percentage. Golden State had to average nearly nine wins for every loss across a schedule that included travel, back-to-backs, injuries, changing matchups, and every opponent treating the defending champions as a major test.

Before **73-9**, the standard was the 1995-96 Bulls at 72-10. Chicago’s record had stood for twenty years and was widely considered almost unreachable. Golden State tied it with its 72nd victory and then secured **73-9** by defeating Memphis in the final regular-season game.

The difference between 72 and 73 sounds small, but improving an already extreme record is difficult. A single upset, tired night, missed shot, or injury could have left the Warriors tied with Chicago instead of placing **73-9** alone at the top of the regular-season standings.

## How the Warriors reached 73-9

The path to **73-9** began with an extraordinary start. Golden State opened the season 24-0. That streak immediately changed the pressure around the team. Every opponent wanted to become the first winner, national attention increased, and each game became part of a growing historical question.

The Warriors did not build **73-9** only by dominating at home. They also won 34 road games, which showed that their success survived travel, hostile crowds, unfamiliar routines, and difficult scheduling. A record like **73-9** requires consistency in ordinary games as well as performance in headline matchups.

Golden State also avoided long slumps. The team never lost consecutive regular-season games. That resilience mattered because one two- or three-game skid could have ended the chase. The final result of **73-9** was not created by one perfect month; it required elite play from October through April.

## Stephen Curry and the 73-9 season

Stephen Curry was the center of the **73-9** Warriors. He became the first unanimous MVP in NBA history, receiving every first-place vote. He led the league in scoring at 30.1 points per game and made 402 three-pointers, the first season in which any player crossed 400.

Curry’s impact on **73-9** was not limited to the number of shots he made. His range changed where defenders had to pick him up. A defense that normally protected the paint first could not simply retreat when Curry was comfortable shooting several feet behind the line.

That gravity opened space for teammates, encouraged fast decisions, and made transition possessions dangerous before the defense could organize. The **73-9** offense could create pressure through pull-up shooting, off-ball movement, screens, quick passes, and cuts rather than relying on one repeated action.

Curry also shot with remarkable efficiency. The combination of volume, range, and accuracy made his **73-9** season different from a typical high-scoring year. It became a model for how an individual skill could reshape an entire team’s geometry.

## Why 73-9 was a team achievement

Although Curry was the MVP, **73-9** was not a one-player record. Klay Thompson supplied elite movement shooting and perimeter defense. Draymond Green provided playmaking, rebounding, switching, communication, and the ability to defend larger players while functioning as an offensive decision-maker.

Andre Iguodala, Harrison Barnes, Andrew Bogut, Shaun Livingston, and the rest of the rotation gave the **73-9** Warriors multiple lineup options. Golden State could play with a traditional center, use a smaller switching group, speed up the game, or execute in the half court.

The famous small lineup with Green at center demonstrated why **73-9** changed roster conversations. It was smaller by listed position but not necessarily weaker. Strength, length, anticipation, passing, shooting, and collective defense allowed the group to gain speed without surrendering every physical matchup.

A team chasing **73-9** cannot depend on five players doing the same job. The Warriors combined complementary skills. One player’s shooting created opportunities for another player’s passing; one defender’s versatility allowed another defender to stay on a difficult assignment.

## How 73-9 changed basketball

The league already valued three-pointers before 2015-16, but **73-9** made shooting volume and spacing impossible to treat as optional trends. The Warriors showed that a championship-level system could use threes as a central resource without becoming predictable.

After **73-9**, teams placed even greater value on guards and wings with extended range, centers who could defend in space, and forwards who could pass after receiving the ball in four-on-three situations. The season did not invent those ideas, but it gave them a record-setting example.

The influence of **73-9** also reached youth basketball and fan culture. Deep pull-up threes became normal highlights. Terms such as gravity, spacing, switching, short-roll playmaking, and lineup versatility became more common in everyday basketball discussion.

Not every attempt to copy **73-9** succeeded. Simply taking more threes did not reproduce Curry’s range, Thompson’s off-ball movement, Green’s two-way playmaking, or the trust and timing of a mature team. The lasting lesson was not “sign five shooters.” It was “build skills that multiply one another.”

## Why the 73-9 Warriors did not win the championship

The most important complication is that the **73-9** Warriors did not win the 2016 NBA championship. Golden State reached the Finals and led Cleveland 3-1, but the Cavaliers won the final three games and took the title.

That ending changes how **73-9** is remembered. It remains the best regular-season record by wins, but it is not a complete championship season. Fans who prioritize the regular-season peak can point to Golden State. Fans who believe the postseason must finish the argument can point to the 72-10 Bulls, who won the 1996 title.

The Finals loss does not erase **73-9**. Eighty-two games still measure months of sustained excellence, and no other team has won 73 regular-season games. At the same time, the loss prevents the record from automatically proving that the Warriors were the greatest team in every sense.

That tension is why **73-9** remains such a powerful basketball topic. The number is factual, but its meaning depends on the question. Best regular-season record? Yes. Greatest complete season? Debatable. Most influential modern offense? A strong case. Best playoff result? No.

## 73-9 compared with the 72-10 Bulls

The 1995-96 Bulls are the unavoidable comparison. Chicago finished 72-10, Michael Jordan won MVP, and the Bulls completed the postseason with a championship. Golden State won one more regular-season game, while Chicago finished the year with the title.

Comparing **73-9** with 72-10 involves different eras, rules, spacing, pace, defensive environments, and roster construction. A direct answer about which team would win a series cannot be proven by the records alone.

The comparison is more useful when it clarifies values. If the question is sustained regular-season wins, **73-9** has the edge. If the question requires both a historic record and a championship, the Bulls have the cleaner case. If the question is influence on modern offensive design, the Warriors’ **73-9** season carries enormous weight.

Both records can remain important without forcing one simple conclusion.

## What 73-9 does and does not prove

**73-9** proves that the 2015-16 Warriors won more regular-season games than any other NBA team. It supports the argument that they were one of the most dominant and influential teams ever.

It does not prove that they would defeat every champion from every era. It does not remove injuries, matchups, coaching, rules, or playoff outcomes from historical comparison. It does not turn one simulation into a definitive answer.

The responsible way to use **73-9** is as a benchmark. It is a clear wall for measuring regular-season excellence and a starting point for discussing how great teams are built.
