A 73-9 run is not five separate player choices. It is one **$100 million portfolio** divided across five lineup positions under uncertain wheel outcomes.

That distinction explains why a roster can contain several excellent names and still simulate poorly. The draft may have purchased talent without preserving enough flexibility to finish the team.

This guide offers a practical budget framework. It is not a list of “must-draft” players, because the available team-seasons change on every run.

## Begin with a spending curve

Before the first spin, choose a rough spending shape. You can break the plan when the market justifies it, but a default protects you from impulse decisions.

Three common shapes are:

### Balanced

Spend a similar amount across most slots, with one moderately expensive anchor. This approach usually preserves flexibility and reduces the chance of a disastrous final position.

### One-star

Use a large share of the budget on one elite player-season, then search for four efficient contracts. This can create a high ceiling, but only when later markets contain real value.

### Stars and minimums

Spend heavily on two or more players and fill the rest with the cheapest legal choices. This is the most fragile structure because the wheel may not produce playable low-cost options for the remaining positions.

New players should usually begin with balanced or one-star builds. The point is not that stars-and-minimums can never work; it is that it depends more heavily on future luck.

## Track the budget per open slot

After every signing, divide the remaining money by the number of open positions.

Suppose you spend $38M on the first player. You have $62M for four slots, or an average of $15.5M each. A second $30M signing leaves $32M for three slots, just over $10M each. The roster now needs three affordable markets in a row.

The average is not a hard maximum. You can spend above it when a player-season is unusually valuable. It is a visibility tool that tells you what the decision demands from future spins.

Write the number mentally before clicking Sign. If the new average feels unrealistic, the contract must be exceptional enough to justify that risk.

## Separate quality from value

A high rating answers “How strong is this player-season in the game?” Price answers “How much flexibility does it consume?” Value combines the two.

The strongest affordable player is not always the best selection. Consider three simplified cases:

- Player A is elite but uses almost half the remaining cap.
- Player B is clearly weaker but costs one third as much.
- Player C is between them in quality and preserves a workable budget.

Player C may produce the strongest final five because the saved money improves two later positions.

This does not mean “always choose cheap players.” Very cheap seasons that add little can waste a limited lineup slot. Look for production that is difficult to replace at a price the complete roster can support.

## Give every expensive contract a job

A premium signing should define what the rest of the lineup needs.

An expensive primary creator may allow later picks to focus on shooting, defense, and rebounding. An expensive scoring center may require low-cost perimeter playmaking. A premium two-way wing can make the remaining positions easier because the player covers multiple needs.

The mistake is buying a star without changing the plan. If the next four picks are evaluated exactly as they would have been in a balanced build, the cap disappears before the roster has a coherent shape.

Before signing an expensive player, complete this sentence: “Because I am paying for this player, the next four slots can be cheaper if they provide _____.”

When the blank has no clear answer, the premium may be based on name recognition rather than roster value.

## Preserve flexibility through the middle picks

The first pick receives the most attention, but the second and third picks often determine whether the draft remains solvable.

After two signings, you should still have more than one way to complete the roster. If only minimum-level contracts can fit, every future spin becomes a pass/fail event. If one remaining position needs a rare type of player, an otherwise good roster may become trapped.

Middle picks are a good place to prioritize:

- Multi-skill player-seasons.
- Reasonable prices rather than absolute ceilings.
- Positions that are difficult to fill cheaply.
- Contracts that leave at least two plausible spending paths.

Flexibility has value even when it does not appear in an individual rating.

## Use re-spins to solve constraints

A re-spin costs budget, so compare it with the actual harm of signing from the current market.

A re-spin is more defensible when:

- No affordable player can fill the position.
- Every option would leave an impossible average for remaining slots.
- The roster already contains the market’s main skill and needs something different.
- A specific position is becoming urgent and the current team-season cannot help.

A re-spin is less defensible when:

- The roster is merely unfamiliar.
- You are chasing a famous name.
- A reasonably priced connector is available.
- The reduced cap would create a larger problem than the current market.

The decision is not “Do I like this team-season?” It is “Is the expected improvement from another market worth the guaranteed cap loss?”

## Plan for position scarcity inside the run

The game’s markets are random, so you cannot know which positions will be cheap later. You can still respond to what the current run reveals.

If several early markets offer affordable guards but no usable bigs, preserving money for power forward and center becomes more important. If a rare high-value center appears at a fair price, taking it may remove a future bottleneck.

Do not apply real-world position scarcity as a permanent rule. Observe the current wheel outcomes. Scarcity is created by the run in front of you.

## Avoid drafting reputation

The asset is a player in one team-season. Read the season and displayed values before the career name.

A legendary player in a late-career season can still be useful, but the price and production should justify the selection. An overlooked role player can be the correct cap decision during an unusually strong year.

A simple test is to hide the name mentally. Would you still sign this combination of price, position, and rating? If not, reputation may be driving the click.

## Complete imperfect drafts

Abandoning a weak-looking run removes the most useful feedback. The result screen compares your lineup with a stronger legal five built from the same team-seasons and budget.

That comparison shows whether the problem was:

- Bad wheel luck.
- Poor value recognition.
- An early cap mistake.
- A missed re-spin.
- An unbalanced spending curve.

A bad result with a small comparison gap can still be a well-played draft. A decent result with a large gap may hide several correctable choices.

Finish the run, record the lesson, and then restart.

## Review one decision at a time

After the simulation, identify the first decision where your roster and the comparison roster diverged. Later differences may simply be consequences of that early choice.

Ask:

1. What did the alternative player cost?
2. How much budget would that have preserved?
3. Which later selection changed because of the saved money?
4. Did the alternative improve one player or the structure of the entire five?

This approach is more useful than comparing only total ratings.

## A repeatable budget checklist

Before each signing:

- How much money remains?
- How many positions are open?
- What is the average budget per open slot?
- Is this player-season expensive because it provides something the lineup truly needs?
- What type of contracts must appear later if I sign now?
- Would a re-spin improve the constraint enough to justify its cost?
- Am I drafting the displayed season or the player’s reputation?

Before simulating:

- Did I spend the full cap productively, or merely spend it?
- Does the lineup have one clear weakness created by the budget?
- Which pick would I reconsider first?
- What result would count as a successful draft given these wheel outcomes?

## The best strategy is adaptable

No fixed allocation wins every run. Random markets make rigid formulas brittle.

A good player starts with a budget shape, updates it when exceptional value appears, and protects enough flexibility to complete the five. The goal is not to avoid every expensive contract. It is to make every expensive contract explain the rest of the roster.
