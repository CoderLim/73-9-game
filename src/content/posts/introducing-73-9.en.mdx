The **73-9 game** is a focused browser basketball strategy game at [73-9.org](https://73-9.org). The challenge is simple to describe and difficult to solve: build a five-player lineup under a **$100 million budget**, then see how it performs against the benchmark created by the **2015-16 Golden State Warriors**.

The site is not a game aggregator and does not embed a catalog of unrelated titles. The playable 73-9 game, methodology pages, strategy guides, and development notes all support the same question: how much historical player-season value can you fit into one constrained lineup?

## Why the 73-9 game targets the Warriors

Golden State won 73 regular-season games and lost 9 in 2015-16, passing the 1995-96 Chicago Bulls’ 72-10 record. Stephen Curry became the first unanimous NBA Most Valuable Player and made 402 three-pointers. The Warriors opened 24-0 and set a record with 34 road wins.

Those facts make the team a recognizable and demanding target. They do not end every historical debate. Golden State lost the 2016 Finals, while the 72-10 Bulls completed their season with a championship. Fans can reasonably weigh regular-season performance, playoff outcome, era, style, and opponent quality differently.

The 73-9 game uses the record as a wall, not as a final verdict on the greatest team. For a complete explanation of the number, read [What is 73-9? The Warriors Historic Season Explained](/blog/what-is-73-9-warriors-historic-season).

## The core draft puzzle

A new 73-9 game run begins with five empty positions and a $100M cap. You do not receive a list of every player in history. Instead, the reel lands on a random franchise-season, and that roster becomes the market for one selection.

From that team-season, you sign one player. The contract reduces the same budget used for all later positions. A re-spin can replace an unattractive market, but it also costs money.

The process repeats until the lineup contains a point guard, shooting guard, small forward, power forward, and center.

This structure creates three connected decisions:

- **Player-season quality:** how strong is the version of the player shown?
- **Price:** how much of the cap does that season consume?
- **Roster consequence:** what options remain for the other positions?

A player can be excellent and still be the wrong signing when the contract makes the rest of the lineup impossible.

## Why the 73-9 game uses player-seasons

Careers flatten important differences. The same player can move from a small role to a peak, change teams, lose minutes, improve efficiency, or age into a different style.

The 73-9 game therefore treats the player in the displayed season as the draft asset. A famous name is not automatically the best choice. A less famous player during a productive season can be more useful when the price leaves enough room to complete the five.

Historical data informs the ratings and prices, but those values are game mechanics. They are not official NBA grades or real salary estimates.

## What happens after the fifth signing

Once the lineup is complete, the 73-9 game runs an 82-game simulation. The result screen presents a season-shaped record and an estimate of how often the roster clears the Warriors benchmark inside the model.

The result is not a real-world prediction. Cross-era basketball includes rule changes, coaching, injuries, pace, defensive schemes, team chemistry, and other factors that no compact browser simulation can reproduce completely.

The simulation is most useful as a consistent comparison tool. Every lineup is evaluated by the same system, so you can compare your own drafts and learn how the budget affected the result.

## The most useful result is the comparison lineup

The 73-9 game also looks back at the exact team-seasons you received and builds a stronger legal lineup under the same $100M cap.

This separates luck from decision-making. Your lineup and the comparison lineup had the same wheel outcomes. The difference comes from which player-season was selected and how the spending fit together.

A large gap may reveal that:

- You paid too much for reputation.
- One early contract forced several weak late picks.
- A cheap value player would have preserved room for a later star.
- The lineup duplicated one type of production instead of balancing the five.

A small gap means your choices were close to the model’s strongest legal combination, even when the overall wheel was difficult.

## Guest play and accounts

The full 73-9 game is available to guests. You do not need an account to spin, draft, simulate, or study the result.

An account is useful when you want an eligible run saved to the public [73-9 game leaderboard](/leaderboard). Account features are optional, and privacy practices are described in the [Privacy Policy](/privacy-policy).

Leaderboard placement cannot be purchased. The current core game does not sell better players, extra simulated wins, or paid rank.

## How advertising should fit

The 73-9 game and editorial content are the reason to visit. Advertising, when enabled, should remain secondary.

Ads should not appear while the core game is showing only a loading state. They should not be placed on sign-in, error, account, empty, or other low-value screens. During advertising review, the ad-serving loader can remain disabled while site ownership is verified through the AdSense account metadata and ads.txt.

Before personalized ads are served in regions that require it, the appropriate Google-certified consent management configuration must be published and tested.

## What comes next

The project will continue to improve in four areas:

1. **Game reliability:** faster loading, clearer errors, and better mobile compatibility.
2. **Data quality:** corrections for player-season records, names, positions, and unusual cases.
3. **Decision feedback:** clearer explanations of why one lineup outperformed another.
4. **Original guides:** useful strategy, history, methodology, and development notes.

Changes that affect how runs are interpreted should be documented rather than silently shipped.
