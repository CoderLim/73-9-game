The result screen is not a single pass-or-fail verdict. It contains several different comparisons, and each answers a different question about the lineup you built.

Reading those parts separately helps you distinguish bad wheel luck from a correctable draft mistake.

## The simulated season record

The season record summarizes how the lineup performs across an 82-game sample inside the 73-9 model.

A strong record means the five performs well against the game’s broader all-era competition. A weaker record suggests the roster lacks enough total strength or balance under the model.

Do not treat the exact wins and losses as a real-world forecast. The simulation cannot reproduce every rule change, coaching system, injury, matchup, or team-chemistry effect. The number is most useful when comparing lineups evaluated under the same game rules.

Two runs with similar Warriors percentages can still have different season records if their broader performance differs.

## The Warriors win percentage

The main historical challenge is the estimated percentage of simulated matchups your lineup wins against the 2015-16 Golden State Warriors benchmark.

A low number means the lineup usually falls short of that specific wall. A higher number means it clears the benchmark more consistently inside repeated simulated matchups.

The percentage should not be read as the probability of winning a real seven-game series. It is an output of the game’s repeated-matchup model. It helps rank your own lineups and evaluate whether a budget decision moved the roster closer to the target.

Because the benchmark is intentionally difficult, a lineup can be very good and still have a modest percentage.

## Average margin

The average margin adds direction and scale to the matchup result.

A negative average margin means the lineup is typically outscored in the simulated Warriors matchup. A positive margin means it usually finishes ahead. A margin near zero indicates a close modelled contest with meaningful game-to-game variation.

Margin can reveal improvement that a rounded win percentage hides. A new draft may still lose most matchups but reduce the average deficit substantially. That is evidence that the roster structure moved in the right direction.

## Spending and lineup totals

The result screen shows how much of the $100M cap you used and summarizes the five player-season values.

Unspent money is not automatically a mistake. The wheel may never offer a useful player at the remaining price. However, a large unused amount can indicate that the draft was too cautious or that a re-spin should have been considered.

Spending the entire cap is also not automatically good. Paying more for the same roster quality does not improve the lineup. The goal is productive spending.

Compare total strength with the spending pattern:

- High strength and nearly full cap: the budget was probably converted efficiently.
- Moderate strength and full cap: one or more contracts may have been overpriced.
- Moderate strength with substantial money left: the roster may have passed on available upgrades.
- Low strength after several re-spins: the cap penalties may have reduced the ability to recover.

## The “best squad you could have signed”

This is the most important learning tool.

The game takes the same team-seasons produced by your spins and searches for a stronger legal lineup under the same $100M limit. The wheel luck is held constant. Only the decisions change.

That means the comparison answers: “Given the opportunities I actually received, how much stronger could the roster have been inside this model?”

A large performance gap does not necessarily mean every selection was wrong. One early contract can change the affordable choices for all later positions.

## Reading the first divergence

Compare your five with the alternative and find the earliest point where the choices differ.

Then reconstruct the budget:

1. How much more or less did the alternative spend?
2. What budget remained after that choice?
3. Which later player became affordable because of the difference?
4. Did the alternative improve a single slot or unlock a better combination across several slots?

The first divergence often explains the rest of the lineup. This is why reviewing decisions chronologically is more useful than comparing only the final names.

## When the comparison gap is small

A small gap suggests your draft was close to the model’s best legal use of those team-seasons.

The overall result may still be weak because the wheel produced difficult markets. That is an important distinction: a poor simulated record can come from limited opportunities rather than poor decisions.

In that situation, the correct lesson may be “finish the run and accept the wheel,” not “change the entire strategy.”

Small gaps are also evidence that your value recognition and budget pacing are improving.

## When the comparison gap is large

A large gap usually points to one of four patterns:

### An expensive early mistake

A premium contract consumed money that the alternative used to improve several positions.

### A missed value season

A cheaper player on one roster created room for a much stronger later signing.

### Reputation bias

The selected name had a stronger career reputation than the season value shown in the current market.

### Re-spin timing

A re-spin was used when the current market contained a workable player, or it was not used when every option created an impossible cap.

Use the gap to identify a specific pattern, not simply to conclude that the roster was bad.

## Comparing runs fairly

When comparing two completed runs, consider more than the headline percentage.

Record:

- Warriors win percentage.
- Average margin.
- Simulated season record.
- Total cap used.
- Number and cost of re-spins.
- Gap to the comparison lineup.
- The first decision you would change.

A lineup with a slightly lower headline percentage but a much smaller comparison gap may represent better drafting from worse wheel outcomes.

## What the result cannot tell you

The screen cannot prove that a historical lineup would perform exactly this way in reality. It does not fully model coaching, chemistry, injuries, modern spacing adjustments, defensive assignments, officiating, or the physical and tactical differences between eras.

It also cannot tell you whether a player was “better” in every context. The model evaluates the selected season within the game’s consistent rules.

The result is a structured prompt for basketball reasoning, not a replacement for film, scouting, or historical analysis.

## Turn the result into the next draft

Choose one lesson before restarting.

Examples:

- Keep at least $12M per open slot through the third pick.
- Use no more than one re-spin unless the roster is impossible.
- Evaluate the displayed season before the player name.
- Preserve extra money for a difficult remaining position.
- Finish every draft and study the comparison lineup.

A specific experiment is easier to evaluate than a vague goal to “do better.”
