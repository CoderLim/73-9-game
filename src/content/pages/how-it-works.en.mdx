## The short version

73-9 turns a famous regular-season benchmark into a repeatable roster-building puzzle. The wheel gives you a sequence of franchise-seasons. You select one player-season from each market, keep the five contracts within a $100M budget, and simulate the completed lineup against the 2015-16 Golden State Warriors.

The game uses historical basketball data to make the choices meaningful, but the output is a designed game model. It is not an official NBA product, a scouting service, a salary calculator, a betting model, or a claim that one cross-era matchup has a provably correct answer.

For the click-by-click rules, begin with [How to play](/how-to-play).

## Why the game drafts player-seasons

A player’s name can cover fifteen or twenty very different years. Production, role, minutes, health, teammates, and style can change dramatically from one season to another. Treating an entire career as one fixed version would erase the detail that makes historical roster building interesting.

73-9 therefore presents players in the context of a specific team-season. The season attached to the spin is part of the asset you are buying. A veteran star in a reduced role is not automatically priced like the same player at a peak, and an overlooked role player can become valuable when the selected season contains unusually strong production.

This season-specific approach also creates more variety. Landing on the same franchise in two different years can produce a completely different market.

## What the historical data contributes

The player pool is built from historical game and season information used by the game runtime. Visible player-season values are informed by categories such as scoring, playmaking, rebounding, shooting, minutes, and other box-score production represented in the dataset.

Historical data provides the raw material; it does not remove design judgment. The game still needs rules for converting different statistical profiles into prices, ratings, lineup strength, and simulated outcomes. Those rules are calibrated for a playable budget challenge, not for academic player valuation.

Several things are deliberately outside the model. Box scores cannot fully capture defensive positioning, screening, off-ball movement, coaching, injury context, rule changes, travel, playoff adjustments, or the way five specific players would learn to play together. The game should be read as a structured comparison, not a complete reconstruction of basketball reality.

## How the $100M economy works

The fixed budget is the central constraint. Without it, the obvious strategy would be to select the strongest available player on every spin. The cap forces opportunity cost: every premium contract weakens your ability to solve the remaining positions.

Player prices translate a season’s in-game value into the same shared budget environment. The number is not the player’s historical salary and is not a forecast of a real contract. It is a game price designed to make seasons comparable inside one run.

The useful question is therefore not “Is this player worth this many real dollars?” It is “Does this player-season add enough to this five for the amount of my game budget it consumes?”

A high price can still be excellent value when the player becomes the lineup’s engine. A low price can be poor value when it fills a slot without adding enough. Price and quality should always be read together.

## How lineup strength is evaluated

After five signings, the game combines the player-season inputs into a team-level estimate. Individual ratings matter, but a lineup is more than one star. The five contracts determine how much high-end production, depth, and positional coverage you managed to purchase.

The model is intentionally consistent: your lineup and the comparison lineup are evaluated under the same rules. This consistency is more important to the game than claiming perfect realism. It lets you compare decisions made from the same wheel outcomes.

The model does not know every tactical interaction. It cannot guarantee that a player listed at one position would defend every opponent, that a historical coach would use the lineup in a specific way, or that personalities and injuries would remain constant. Those uncertainties are why the result is framed as a simulation rather than a factual prediction.

## What the 82-game simulation represents

An 82-game sample turns lineup strength into a season-shaped result. Instead of presenting one deterministic matchup, the game introduces variation and reports how the roster performs across repeated games.

The simulated record gives you an intuitive summary. The Warriors comparison then asks a more difficult question: how often does this roster clear the benchmark set by the 73-9 team?

A percentage near the middle does not mean the teams would literally split a real series. It means the game model sees meaningful uncertainty across repeated simulated matchups. A very low percentage means the lineup usually falls short under the model; a higher percentage means the lineup more consistently reaches the game’s historical target.

The result is best used comparatively. Compare your own runs, compare different spending structures, and compare your roster with the legal alternative generated from the same markets.

## Why the 2015-16 Warriors are the benchmark

The Warriors finished 73-9, surpassing the 1995-96 Chicago Bulls’ 72-10 regular-season record. Stephen Curry became the first unanimous NBA Most Valuable Player and made 402 three-pointers that season. The team opened 24-0 and set a then-record with 34 road wins.

Those achievements make the team a recognizable target, but the game is not arguing that regular-season wins settle every “greatest team” debate. Golden State did not win the 2016 championship, while the 72-10 Bulls completed their season with a title. Different fans can reasonably value peak record, playoff outcome, era, matchup, or longevity differently.

73-9 uses the Warriors because the number creates a clear, difficult wall. The game turns that wall into a budget problem rather than trying to end the historical debate.

## How the comparison lineup works

When a run finishes, the game looks back at the team-seasons you were offered and identifies a stronger legal lineup it could build under the same $100M constraint.

This feature separates two sources of performance:

- **Wheel outcomes:** which team-seasons appeared.
- **Draft decisions:** which player you selected from each market and how the spending fit together.

The comparison roster cannot change your spins. It only makes different choices from the same opportunity set. That is why it is the most useful diagnostic on the result screen.

A small gap suggests your choices were close to the model’s best legal combination. A large gap often points to one expensive mistake, a missed value season, or a budget curve that became too restrictive.

## What the model does not claim

73-9 does not claim to:

- Predict a real game, playoff series, or future season.
- Provide official player ratings or historical salary values.
- Account for every rule change or era-specific playing environment.
- Replace film study, scouting, or advanced analytics.
- Offer gambling, fantasy-sports, or financial advice.
- Prove that one historical lineup is objectively greater than another.

The model is useful because it is transparent about its purpose: creating a fair, replayable decision game from a consistent historical dataset.

## Data quality and corrections

Historical sports datasets can contain missing values, naming variations, traded-player records, position ambiguity, or unusual small-sample seasons. The game applies validation and aggregation rules, but errors are still possible.

When you notice a player-season that looks incorrect, send the player name, team-season, displayed value, and a screenshot to [support@73-9.org](mailto:support@73-9.org). Specific reports are easier to investigate than general impressions.

Game balance may also change. If a pricing rule, dataset, or simulation parameter is updated, old and new runs may not be perfectly comparable. Important changes should be recorded in the [blog](/blog).
